About

How I work

I work in the gap between customers, product and providers.

I have spent most of the last nine years running support and community operations for crypto products. Most recently I was a Customer Support Engineer at Exodus, where I became the team's go-to person for fiat-to-crypto transactions and the escalations that involve external payment providers.

That seam, where a customer sees one thing, support sees another, and the provider's system says something else, is where I do my best work. Failures there are easy for each side to miss on its own.

Before that I led a 16-person remote community team at AmaZix, and before that I was a site civil engineer in Port Harcourt. I am currently completing an MBA at Lagos Business School.

  • Follow-through. I take on shared, unclear work and keep pushing it until it is resolved, without waiting to be asked.
  • Leverage. I build guides, tests and workflows so the work scales past me instead of routing through me.
  • The seam. I sit between customers, support, product and external providers, and I can see failures that neither side sees alone.

Selected work

Exodus, 2022 to 2026

Problems I picked up and carried to the end.

Figures marked "estimated" are my own estimates from the time, not numbers from a report.

01

The 200-day order problem

Problem. More than 200 orders had failed at a payment provider but still showed as "in progress" in the app, and the issue had been open for over 200 days.

What I did. Investigated the status discrepancy and kept following up with the provider until it reconciled the orders.

Result. 200+ orders displayed correctly. I also got a provider to expose the statuses needed for sell orders with revised price quotes, which engineering then showed in the app.

02

Limiting the damage from provider outages

Problem. When a payment provider fails, customers keep trying and keep failing.

What I did. Monitored orders and took failing fiat providers offline through remote configuration, typically within about 30 to 40 minutes when no quick fix was likely. On swap-monitoring duty I disabled failing swap providers immediately. I coordinated engineering, support and providers through recovery.

Result. Less customer exposure to outages. Timings are recalled operational practice, not a formal SLA.

03

Weekly end-to-end purchase testing

Problem. Payment-flow bugs were reaching customers before anyone caught them.

What I did. Helped build and run a weekly test cycle: wrote the scripts, briefed about 5 testers, triaged an estimated 20 to 25 results per cycle and escalated bugs and usability issues to engineering and providers.

Result. A payment-method and provider mapping fix, a proposed correction for a separate wallet transaction discrepancy, and data-backed rollout advice for 4 new providers.

04

Fewer questions, because the answers existed

Purchase limits. I collated recurring feedback, mapped the manual verification process and pushed engineering to replace a vague limit error with clear in-app messaging. Limit-related inquiries dropped by an estimated 50% week on week. I also proposed an automated process for granting higher limits.

Guides and procedures. Guides for compliance flags and order errors contributed to an estimated 80%+ drop in specialist pings from frontline support over a month.

05

Claude-powered support workflows

What I built. Workflows that turn Slack case triage into task lists and search Slack, GitHub and Notion for context, so I could answer frontline questions without waiting on engineers. Plus a twice-weekly digest that translates pull requests across 4+ GitHub repositories into plain-language guidance on user impact.

Result. An estimated 50% cut in my own investigation time on engineering-related questions. The digest fed most of the weekly fiat product-operations report to leadership, and I shared the tooling with colleagues.

06

Auditing a support-dashboard migration

Problem. The team was moving to a new payment-support dashboard, and nobody had compared it with the old one.

What I did. Audited the outgoing tool against its replacement, then wrote an internal reference guide to its fields and the steps for investigating failed orders and account issues.

Result. 9 gaps, 12 new capabilities and 9 recommendations documented before the migration, including missing risk-score and identity-verification fields needed to diagnose blocked users.
